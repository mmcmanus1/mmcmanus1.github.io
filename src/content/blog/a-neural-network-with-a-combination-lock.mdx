You type a phrase into a neural network. It returns zero. You try another phrase. Zero again.

You have all the weights. Every layer is available to inspect. Surely that should make finding a successful input easier?

The interesting part of [Jane Street’s tensor puzzle](https://blog.janestreet.com/can-you-reverse-engineer-our-neural-network/) is the gap between those two things: having a complete description of a computation, and knowing how to make it do what you want. The network was constructed by hand, rather than trained to understand the phrase. We're going to take apart its final test first, then work out what feeds into it.

This post now includes an executable solution: extract the target from the original weights, search English word pairs, and run the resulting phrase through the complete network. The implementation and verification are new; the MD5 interpretation and answer were already published. The mechanism is spoiled below, but the winning phrase stays behind a reveal. [Download the code and measured results](#run-it-yourself) to reproduce the run.

## Start with the lock, not the key

Before naming thousands of moving parts, I'd look at what makes the machine's green light turn on. The last layers compare sixteen computed values with sixteen fixed targets.

For this puzzle, think of a **layer** as a row of little calculators. Each calculator takes numbers from the previous row, multiplies them by fixed numbers called **weights**, adds an offset called a **bias**, and passes the result onward. Many of these calculators also do one very simple thing: replace a negative answer with zero.

A ReLU takes a number and discards its negative part:

$$
R(x)=\max(0,x).
$$

For example, $R(-3)=0$, $R(0)=0$, and $R(2)=2$. Its graph is flat to the left of zero and a rising ramp to the right. Nothing about that looks like a test for equality.

Let's build one. Call the difference between the computed value and its target $d$. A target of 42 and a computed value of 43 give $d=1$. We want a little peak at zero, with a flat floor on either side.

Start a ramp at $-1$. When you reach zero, subtract a ramp that's twice as steep: the line folds downward. When you reach one, add another ramp to stop the descent. Try those three moves below before reading the finished formula.

<Explorable kind="relu-build" />

Now the notation is just a compact record of what you built:

$$
E(d)=R(d-1)-2R(d)+R(d+1).
$$

Here $d=v-t$, where $v$ is the computed value and $t$ is the target. The order of the three terms doesn't change their sum.

At zero, the three ramps contribute $0-0+1=1$. At one, they contribute $0-2+2=0$. Farther to the right, their slopes cancel. Far enough to the left, none has switched on.

The result is a little tent:

$$
E(d)=\max(0,1-|d|).
$$

On the real line, this gives partial credit for being nearby. Move the probe to $d=0.25$ at step 3: the score is $0.75$.

But the values being compared are **bytes**. A bit is a zero or a one; a byte is a group of eight bits, representing an integer from 0 through 255. For example, the byte `00101010` represents $32+8+2=42$. We aren't comparing arbitrary fractions here.

There is no integer strictly between zero and one. Move the probe through the integers $-2,-1,0,1,2$: only zero sits on the peak. On that grid, the tent becomes an exact equality test:

$$
E(v-t)=
\begin{cases}
1,&v=t,\\
0,&v\ne t.
\end{cases}
$$

That's the first useful change of perspective. A neuron needn't represent a fuzzy concept. A few neurons can implement an ordinary program instruction.

<details>
<summary>Why those particular coefficients?</summary>

Look at the slope as you move from left to right. At $d=-1$, the $R(d+1)$ term starts climbing: slope $+1$. At $d=0$, the $-2R(d)$ term switches on: total slope $-1$. At $d=1$, the $R(d-1)$ term joins: total slope zero. Three changes of slope make the tent. The coefficients are a recipe for its corners.

</details>

## Sixteen tumblers, one green light

We can now check one byte. What should happen when fifteen of the sixteen bytes match? Make a prediction, then try changing individual matches.

<ByteLock />

The distinction is between **averaging** sixteen checks and **requiring** all sixteen. An average would award partial credit. This circuit doesn't.

Let $v_i$ and $t_i$ be byte number $i$ of the computed result and the target. Sum the sixteen equality scores, subtract fifteen, and apply another ReLU. The large $\sum$ symbol below just means “add this score for each of the sixteen positions”:

$$
F(v)=R\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{16}E(v_i-t_i)-15\right).
$$

Sixteen matches give $R(1)=1$. Fifteen matches give $R(0)=0$. Fewer matches also give zero. This is an AND gate: every tumbler must line up.

The final linear layer's repeated $1,-2,1$ groups and bias $-15$ are the fingerprints of that construction. [Thomas Brownback’s independent write-up](https://thomasbrownback.com/20260711.html) documents those weights and the target digest he recovered.

We've identified the lock. Later, we'll inspect what supplies its sixteen values.

## The shrinking target

Let's briefly leave the actual integer inputs behind. Imagine we could directly adjust the computed bytes by fractional amounts. Could we follow an uphill slope toward a larger output?

That's the intuition behind **gradient ascent**. If moving a knob a little to the right increases the score, move right. If it decreases the score, move left. A derivative measures that local slope; a gradient collects the slopes for many knobs. Backpropagation is a way to calculate them through the layers of a network.

“Local” is the important word. A perfectly flat patch tells you neither which direction the peak lies nor how far away it is. A zero slope isn't evidence that no better point exists.

Keep fifteen comparisons exactly right, and miss the last by $0.1$. The final output is $0.9$. That seems encouraging.

Now miss **all sixteen** by the same $0.1$. Each individual test scores $0.9$. But their sum is only $14.4$, below the final threshold of fifteen. The output is zero.

More generally, if $k$ comparisons share an error $d$ and the others are perfect, then

$$
F_k(d)=\max(0,1-k|d|).
$$

<Explorable kind="relu-lock" />

With sixteen imperfect comparisons, a positive output requires $|d|<1/16$. The slope inside that narrow tent is steep. Outside it, the slope is zero. Increasing the steepness hasn't made the useful region easier to find.

Try following the slope on a wider tent, with four shared errors. Start inside, then outside. Does the same rule work in both places?

<SlopeWalk />

These plots live in the space of computed byte values, not text inputs. Editing a phrase doesn't let us move one byte independently. We'll see that second obstacle next.

<details>
<summary>Optional: unequal errors and the geometry of the target</summary>

We can also drop the assumption that the errors are equal. Substituting the tent formula gives

$$
F(v)=\max\!\left(0,1-\sum_{i=1}^{16}\min(1,|v_i-t_i|)\right).
$$

For this to be positive, every error must be smaller than one, and the **sum** of their absolute sizes must be smaller than one. Being close in each coordinate isn't enough; they share a single error budget.

With two errors, the positive region is $|d_1|+|d_2|<1$: a diamond inside the square $[-1,1]^2$. In $n$ dimensions, it occupies a fraction $1/n!$ of the corresponding cube. Each sign choice contributes a simplex of volume $1/n!$, giving total volume $2^n/n!$ versus cube volume $2^n$.

For sixteen dimensions that fraction is about $4.78\times10^{-14}$. This is an exact volume ratio for the thought experiment—not a measured chance of solving the puzzle, and not an assumption that actual network activations are uniformly distributed in that cube.

</details>

## Recognizing the machine behind the lock

The hidden computation is MD5, a **hash function**. It deterministically turns a message into a fixed-size label called a **digest**. The same message gives the same label every time. The label isn't a summary of the message's meaning, and you don't decode it to get the message back.

MD5's label is 128 bits, or sixteen bytes. To print it compactly, we write each byte as two hexadecimal digits. Hexadecimal uses sixteen symbols, `0–9` and `a–f`; for example, decimal 42 becomes `2a`. Those long strings of letters and numbers are simply another way to write the sixteen integers going into our lock. The [MD5 specification](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1321) describes the computation that produces them.

Before thinking about finding the winning phrase, try a smaller question: if you change just one letter, how much of the label changes?

<HashBytes />

The important separation is now visible. We could toggle one comparison in the lock above without disturbing the others. Editing the text doesn't give us that control: its effects can spread across the digest.

To test that identification, you would use several known strings, extract the candidate digest immediately before the comparison, and check it against an ordinary MD5 implementation. Compare all sixteen bytes, not merely the model's final zero. A zero throws away almost everything you need to identify the computation.

<details>
<summary>Evidence checkpoint: what would actually convince us?</summary>

**A clue:** sixteen bytes is the right output size for MD5. But plenty of programs produce sixteen bytes. Size alone doesn't identify the algorithm.

**A testable hypothesis:** the values just before the equality circuit are the input's MD5 digest. Predict all sixteen bytes for several chosen inputs, then compare them with extracted activations. Agreement is stronger evidence than matching a shape or a layer count.

**A boundary check:** try inputs of different lengths and check the input encoding. A few successful examples don't prove agreement everywhere. The reported length bug is exactly why that distinction matters.

## Why a better score isn't necessarily a better phrase

You might try removing the last ReLU. Then the raw score becomes

$$
S(s)=\text{number of matching digest bytes}-15.
$$

This reveals partial matches. But it doesn't explain how to improve them. A phrase matching three bytes isn't necessarily a useful starting point for finding one that matches four.

Here's a useful null model—not an experiment on the original network. Imagine drawing each of sixteen bytes independently from 0 through 255, with every value equally likely. Each has a $1/256$ chance of matching its target. The number of matches $M$ follows a **binomial distribution**: the count of successes in sixteen independent tries, each with the same success probability.

$$
M\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(16,1/256).
$$

It's easier to count the opposite of “at least one”: no matches at all. One byte misses with probability $255/256$. All sixteen miss with probability $(255/256)^{16}$. Subtract that from one:

$$
P(M\ge1)=1-(255/256)^{16}\approx6.07\%.
$$

So among a million random-looking digests, you'd expect roughly 60,702 to improve on the usual raw score of $-15$. That sounds like thousands of promising leads. Yet the model assigns no special significance to the words in them. Accidental agreement is common enough to look like progress.

Partial matches become useful leads only if they help choose the next phrase. This calculation doesn't give us that link.

## Finding the key

The intended candidate space is much smaller than all possible strings: two lowercase English words separated by a space. Jane Street explains that this restriction was hinted at in the puzzle. With a dictionary of $N$ words, testing every ordered pair takes $N^2$ hashes. [Puzzle and solution discussion](https://blog.janestreet.com/can-you-reverse-engineer-our-neural-network/).

That can still be substantial work. More importantly, a dictionary that omits either word cannot succeed, however fast you search it. The assumptions defining your candidate set matter as much as the search algorithm.

This is a constrained **preimage search**: find a message with a particular hash. It is different from finding any two messages with the same hash, called a collision. Recognizing MD5 doesn't magically supply the missing message; the clue about the message space is what makes enumeration plausible.

<details>
<summary>Reveal the phrase and a reproducible check</summary>

The published solution gives **bitter lesson**: lowercase, one space, no trailing newline. Credit to [Shreyas Mahimkar’s write-up and linked code](https://medium.com/@shreyasmahimkar/reverse-engineering-a-neural-network-md5-hash-puzzle-848c82ed9285).

This check uses Python's standard library:

```python
import hashlib

phrase = "bitter lesson"
target = "c7ef65233c40aa32c2b9ace37595fa7c"
digest = hashlib.md5(
    phrase.encode("ascii"), usedforsecurity=False
).hexdigest()

assert digest == target
print(digest)
```

The attached solver also finds this phrase by enumeration and runs it through the original network: output **1.0**. Capitalizing both words, appending a space or newline, or doubling the internal space each produces **0.0**. Those are measured model outputs. Case, spaces, and newlines are bytes too—changing them changes what you hash.

</details>

## Run it yourself

On September 7, 2026, I used Codex to implement and execute the published approach against Jane Street's original `model.pt`. The run recovered the target from the weights, searched **48,396,178 word pairs**, and verified the resulting phrase through all **5,442 layers** of the network. The search took **20.81 seconds** on this Mac using one Python process; that timing excludes downloading and loading the model and running the verification probes.

[Download the complete solution bundle](/downloads/jane-street/jane-street-solution.zip), or inspect the [Python solver](/downloads/jane-street/solve.py), [run instructions](/downloads/jane-street/README.md), [dependencies](/downloads/jane-street/requirements.txt), and [actual results JSON](/downloads/jane-street/results.json). **The results and bundle contain the answer.** The 1.16 GB model is downloaded separately from Jane Street; it is not included in the bundle.

The dictionary comes from the first 10,000 English entries in `wordfreq` 3.1.1. Filtering to lowercase ASCII words leaves **9,842 words**, in their original frequency order. The search tests ordered pairs up to 31 bytes long. Neither the winning phrase nor the target digest is hardcoded in the solver, and neither word is specially inserted. I knew the published answer before writing the program; reproducing the search does not make this a blind discovery.

### Reading actual activations

There is a useful implementation detail hidden by the simplified diagrams: the penultimate linear layer has **192 inputs and 48 outputs**. Its three repeated weight blocks each turn those 192 activations into sixteen byte values. Taking the first block's matrix product gives the digest before the biases and ReLU discard information:

```python
# comparator is the penultimate Linear layer from the loaded model.
projection = comparator.weight.detach()[:16]
target = -comparator.bias.detach()[16:32]

# In a forward pre-hook on that layer:
network_digest = projection @ inputs[0]
```

The solver checks that all three weight blocks agree, that their biases differ by one, and that the final layer has the expected weights and bias. It then runs the empty string, `a`, `abc`, `hello world`, and `vegetable dog`. Every extracted digest agrees byte for byte with Python's MD5. For example, the actual network produces `900150983cd24fb0d6963f7d28e17f72` for `abc` and then outputs zero because that digest does not match the target.

### Reproducing the boundary bug

The same measurement catches the length bug. Repeated `a` inputs match standard MD5 at lengths 30 and 31, but diverge at **32**, 33, and 55. At 32 characters, the network produces `3b6712e7d9d6911ac23fd69d2ede6abd`; Python's MD5 produces `5eca9bd3eb07c006cd43ae48dfde7fd3`. Both still fail the final lock. Looking only at the output zero would conceal this difference.

The bundle includes Python 3.10 setup instructions, a pinned model download URL, and the expected SHA-256 checksum. The program checks that checksum before loading the model, then writes its environment, dictionary checksum, search count, and all fifteen model evaluations to JSON. Its assertions fail if the recovered phrase is not accepted, the altered phrases are accepted, or the digest checks disagree with the recorded expectations.

## What this puzzle does—and doesn't—show

We worked backward from an acceptance condition, recognized a known computation, and used a clue to narrow the search. Understanding the program and finding an input it accepts were different jobs.

This isn't a proof that gradients are useless or that learned networks are impossible to interpret. The obstacles here are specific: an all-or-nothing gate and a hash between our editable phrase and the values we want to change.

My favorite lesson is practical. Before improving the optimizer, check whether you're optimizing a score that knows what progress looks like.

<details>
<summary>Quick check: is a fifteen-byte match almost a solution?</summary>

It is close in digest coordinates: one comparison is wrong. It need not be close in phrase coordinates: a one-character edit need not preserve those fifteen matches. “Almost” is meaningful only after you say which space you're measuring it in.

</details>

One last thought experiment: **what if the secret were a random 20-character string instead of two English words?** Suppose we kept the same construction but replaced the target digest with that string's hash. Would understanding the network still give us a practical solution?

<details>
<summary>Think it through, then compare</summary>

The lock would work exactly as before. We could still recognize MD5 and extract the target. What disappears is the useful clue about which inputs to try.

Even restricting the secret to twenty independently chosen, uniformly random lowercase letters gives $26^{20}$ possible strings. A two-word dictionary search no longer covers the intended secret space. Knowing how to check a guess doesn't tell us which guess to make next.

This isn't a proof that every possible attack must enumerate that space. It's the narrower point that our practical dictionary shortcut depended on how the secret was chosen, not just on understanding the network.

Same mechanism. Same explanation. A very different search problem.

</details>
